# The state of machine-to-machine security

Machine-to-machine security is in bad shape, and the evidence for that is not hard to find. The same concerns turn up across academic papers, industry reports, and security write-ups going back years and running through 2025 and into 2026. M2M communication is what sits underneath IoT, industrial IoT, smart infrastructure, and most automated systems, and much of it was built under constraints that make solid security hard to reach once you scale past a handful of devices.

The weak points are consistent from one source to the next. A lot of devices ship with default or weak credentials, verify identity poorly, or skip authentication altogether, which leaves the door open to anyone who bothers to try. Plenty of traffic still moves unencrypted or over dated protocols, so interception and man-in-the-middle attacks stay easy. MQTT and protocols like it carry known flaws that attackers have turned into denial of service, code execution, reconnaissance, and lateral movement. The devices themselves usually cannot do much better: limited compute, memory, and power rule out strong cryptography and regular patching. The field never settled on common standards either, so MQTT, CoAP, Zigbee, and a stack of vendor variants each need their own security handling, and every seam between them is a place something can go wrong. Many devices are physically reachable, run old firmware with holes that were never closed, and have no safe way to take an update. On top of all that, there are simply too many of them to patch, monitor, and retire in any orderly way. More recently, adversarial AI has entered the picture, with model poisoning and data manipulation now threatening the automated authentication and decision-making that M2M systems lean on.

These points show up again and again in IEEE papers, vendor analyses from the likes of Trend Micro and Veridify, market reports, and a fresh crop of 2025 publications.

## What happens when the link is compromised

When an M2M link is broken into, the fallout ranges well beyond stolen records. Data breaches are the obvious case, but compromised links also cause operational disruption, physical damage in industrial settings, and real safety risks where medical devices or critical infrastructure are involved. Captured devices also get folded into botnets and pointed at DDoS targets. This is concrete enough that market analyses tracking the growth of M2M connections name cybersecurity as one of the main things holding adoption back, even while the number of connections keeps climbing.

The picture has not changed much heading into 2026. Articles through 2025 keep flagging the same industrial IoT problems: unauthorized access, interception, and insecure protocols. The AI-driven threats to autonomous M2M are newer but drawing steady attention. Fixes do exist, including better PKI, certificate-based authentication, encryption, and purpose-built platforms, but they are far from universal, and a lot of deployments are still running without them.

## Breaches that actually happened

The history backs up the pattern. Most of these incidents trace to the same handful of causes: weak or default credentials, unencrypted protocols, compromised supply chains, and devices that never got patched.

The Mirai botnet from 2016 is the one everyone points to first. The malware scanned the internet for IoT devices with default or weak Telnet credentials, cameras, routers, and DVRs mostly, infected them, and assembled a botnet large enough to drive major DDoS attacks. It ran entirely on poor M2M authentication and the absence of updates. Variants kept the approach alive for years afterward, including BadBox 2.0 in 2025, which reached more than ten million smart TVs and infotainment systems either preinstalled or through later infection, along with others like Matrix, Raptor Train, and Kimwolf, all building botnets for DDoS, proxy services, and fraud.

A few other cases from that era are worth naming. In 2013, attackers stole credentials from a third-party HVAC vendor whose sensors talked to Target's network for energy monitoring, then used that foothold to move into the main systems and lift millions of credit card numbers, which shows how a peripheral M2M link becomes a way in. In 2015, researchers took over a Jeep Cherokee's cellular-connected infotainment system and pivoted from there to the steering, brakes, and engine. In 2017, more than 465,000 implantable St. Jude Medical pacemakers were found vulnerable through insecure wireless communication, where an attacker could in principle change how the device behaved or run its battery down.

The more recent incidents follow the same script. In 2021, someone reached the human-machine interface at a Florida water treatment plant, itself tied to sensors and PLCs, and tried to spike the sodium hydroxide to dangerous levels before an operator caught it. In 2025, a cloud misconfiguration at Mars Hydro exposed 2.7 billion records from connected grow-light devices, including Wi-Fi credentials, IP addresses, and device IDs, all of it down to weak integration between the devices and the cloud. That same year, the BadBox 2.0 botnet came to light as the largest known TV botnet, built on malware preinstalled across millions of Android-based smart TVs, projectors, and in-car systems that phoned home to command-and-control servers for enrollment and then ran fraud, credential stuffing, and DDoS, a supply-chain problem as much as a device one. In 2024, the IoT and M2M connectivity provider ORBCOMM had more than 70 TB of data exposed, covering device telemetry, tracking, and communications across a range of industries. And through 2025, attackers abused stolen long-lived OAuth tokens in SaaS-to-SaaS integrations, as in the Salesforce and Gainsight or Drift incidents, to impersonate trusted workloads, pull out data, and move sideways, a reminder that machine identities in automated communication carry a wide blast radius once they are compromised.

## The short version

Set against how large the attack surface is and how much rides on these systems, M2M security is widely judged inadequate. Progress is real, with better protocols and zero-trust approaches for machines gaining ground, but in a lot of live deployments, especially the legacy and industrial ones, the security is still weak and still lagging behind the threat.
